## How Typing Speed Is Measured

Before comparing numbers, it helps to understand what those numbers actually mean.

**WPM (words per minute)** doesn't count individual words — it counts characters. The universal standard defines one "word" as **five characters**, including spaces and punctuation. So if you type the phrase "I love to type" — that's 14 characters, including the spaces — it counts as 2.8 words for WPM purposes, not 4.

The formula is straightforward:

> **WPM = (Total characters typed ÷ 5) ÷ Minutes elapsed**

For example: if you type 1,500 characters in 5 minutes, that's 300 characters per minute, which equals **60 WPM**.

You might also see **CPM (characters per minute)** on some platforms. To convert: divide CPM by 5 to get WPM, or multiply WPM by 5 to get CPM. A 60 WPM typist types 300 CPM.

Some job postings — especially in data entry and government administration — list requirements in **KPH (keystrokes per hour)**. To convert to WPM: divide KPH by 300. So a job requiring 18,000 KPH is asking for 60 WPM.

## WPM Benchmarks by Skill Level

Here's the clearest way to interpret any typing speed: where does it fall on the skill spectrum?

| Skill Tier | WPM Range | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner | 10–25 WPM | Hunt-and-peck, looking at keyboard frequently |
| Below Average | 26–38 WPM | Some familiarity, no consistent technique |
| Average | 39–55 WPM | Most untrained adults who type regularly |
| Above Average | 56–70 WPM | Comfortable touch typist, office-ready |
| Proficient | 71–90 WPM | Experienced typist, competitive for most jobs |
| Advanced | 91–110 WPM | Top-tier for standard keyboard typists |
| Expert | 111–130 WPM | Competitive typist, achieved through sustained training |
| World-Class | 130+ WPM | Less than 1% of typists; competition-level |

<br>

> **Where do most people actually fall?** Studies consistently place the global average adult typing speed between 38 and 44 WPM. A 2023 Aalto University analysis of over 136,000 typists found a median of around 52 WPM — but that group was self-selected (people who sought out a typing test tend to type more than average). The true general-population average is likely closer to 38–42 WPM.

The number to keep in mind: **anything above 60 WPM puts you ahead of the majority of desk workers**. Above 80 WPM, you're at the level expected of professional typists and journalism graduates. Above 100 WPM, you're in the top few percent of all typists worldwide.

## Average Typing Speed by Age Group

Age has a measurable effect on typing speed — but probably not in the way you'd expect.

Raw speed peaks in young adulthood. That's the period when motor learning is fastest, screen time is highest, and the sheer volume of typing (academic work, social media, gaming) drives rapid improvement. But older typists often have a significant edge in **accuracy and consistency** — they make fewer mistakes and require less correction time, which matters in real-world work.

Here's how typing speed typically breaks down by age:

| Age Group | Average WPM Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Children (6–11) | 5–20 WPM | Learning fundamentals; keyboarding in school |
| Early Teens (12–14) | 20–40 WPM | Rapid growth; heavy social media and gaming use |
| Teenagers (15–19) | 35–60 WPM | Often fast but accuracy varies widely |
| Young Adults (20–30) | 55–80 WPM | Peak raw speed; high screen time and academic/professional use |
| Adults (31–45) | 45–70 WPM | Speed stable or slightly reduced; accuracy often improves |
| Adults (46–60) | 38–60 WPM | Gradual slowdown begins; precision remains strong |
| Seniors (61–75) | 28–50 WPM | Speed lower, but error rates often better than younger typists |
| Seniors (75+) | 20–35 WPM | Motor slowdown more significant; accuracy-focused |

<br>

A few things worth noting about this data:

**Speed doesn't tell the whole story at any age.** Research consistently shows that younger typists — particularly teens and young adults — type faster on average but make substantially more errors. Older adults tend to produce cleaner text, which often means their *effective* output is closer to younger typists than raw WPM suggests.

**The peak years are 18–30, driven by specific factors.** This period combines high neural plasticity (the ability to form new motor patterns quickly) with the highest total typing volume for most people. After 30, speed tends to plateau rather than drop sharply — and for people who type professionally every day, speed often continues to improve well into their 40s.

**You can improve at any age.** Typing speed is a motor skill, and motor skills respond to deliberate practice at any age. Adults in their 50s and 60s who take up structured typing practice regularly make meaningful gains, even if their ceiling is somewhat lower than it would be at 25.

## What About Accuracy?

Speed without accuracy is nearly useless in professional contexts.

A typist at 70 WPM with 90% accuracy is producing 7 errors per 100 words. In a 1,000-word document, that's 70 errors to find and fix. In many cases, that error-correction overhead makes them slower in *effective* output than a 55 WPM typist at 98% accuracy who barely needs to edit.

This is why most professional typing standards list **both** a WPM minimum and an accuracy requirement. The most common accuracy benchmark across professions is **95%**, with many legal and medical roles requiring 98–99%.

The practical takeaway: **when evaluating your typing speed, always note your accuracy alongside your WPM.** A "clean" 60 WPM (at 97% accuracy) is more valuable than a "noisy" 75 WPM (at 88% accuracy) in almost any real-world context.

## Gross WPM vs. Net WPM

You'll see both terms on typing platforms. Here's the difference:

**Gross WPM** counts every character you typed, including incorrect ones, divided by the standard formula. It's your raw speed, ignoring errors.

**Net WPM** subtracts a penalty for errors — typically one word per error — from your gross WPM. It reflects your *productive* typing speed: how much usable output you actually generated.

> **Net WPM = Gross WPM − (Errors ÷ Time in minutes)**

Most professional typing tests and job assessments use Net WPM, because that's what actually matters in practice. If your typing platform only shows one number, check whether it's penalizing errors. If it isn't, your real effective speed may be lower than the displayed number suggests.

For practice purposes, tracking both numbers is useful. If your gross WPM is significantly higher than your net WPM, that's a signal: you're typing fast but inaccurately, and slowing down slightly to improve accuracy would likely raise your effective output.

## Is Your Typing Speed Holding You Back?

A useful framework: typing speed becomes a meaningful bottleneck when your fingers can't keep pace with your thinking.

Here's a quick self-assessment:

**Typing speed is not your bottleneck if:**
- You type mostly casual messages and short emails
- You spend more time thinking than typing in your work
- You feel no frustration when transcribing your thoughts
- Your job doesn't require sustained document production

**Typing speed is likely a bottleneck if:**
- You lose your train of thought while typing because the physical act is too slow
- You feel rushed during timed tasks (exams, live note-taking, customer chat)
- You finish work sessions feeling like typing was effortful rather than automatic
- Job postings you're targeting list minimum WPM requirements you don't currently meet
- You type the same things repeatedly and wish it were faster

A general threshold to keep in mind: research consistently finds that above **70–80 WPM**, typing speed stops being a meaningful constraint for most knowledge workers. At that level, the bottleneck shifts from fingers to thinking — and that's exactly where it should be. Below that threshold, slow typing creates real friction that compounds across an entire workday.

## How to Find Your Current WPM

Before you can improve, you need an accurate baseline.

A few important notes on getting a reliable number:

**Take multiple tests, not just one.** A single typing test has high variance — you might catch yourself on an exceptionally good or bad run. Take 3–5 tests and use your average. Your average of multiple tests is a far more honest picture of your actual speed than your single best score.

**Use a consistent test length.** Most casual tests default to one minute. This is useful for quick benchmarks but doesn't reflect endurance. If your work involves sustained typing, also test yourself at 2–5 minutes to see how your speed holds up over time.

**Don't "practice" the test.** Take the test with text you haven't seen before. Re-reading the same passage skews your score upward because you're partially typing from short-term memory.

**Note both numbers.** Record both your WPM and your accuracy percentage every time you test. You need both to understand your actual performance.

## How to Improve Your Typing Speed

Once you know your benchmark, improving it is mostly a matter of consistent, deliberate practice applied to the right areas.

The highest-leverage changes for most typists:

**If you're under 40 WPM:** The priority is technique, not speed drills. Most typists in this range are still hunt-and-pecking or using two to four fingers. Switching to proper touch typing — all ten fingers, eyes on the screen — will double or triple your speed ceiling before speed drills even become relevant. Start with [structured typing lessons](/typing-lessons) and commit to not looking at the keyboard.

**If you're between 40 and 60 WPM:** You likely have the basics of touch typing but muscle memory isn't fully automated yet. Targeted [typing practice](/typing-practice) on your weak keys and common word patterns will drive the next phase of improvement. Focus on accuracy: most people in this range make more errors than they realize.

**If you're between 60 and 80 WPM:** You're a solid typist. Getting to 80+ requires a combination of speed drills (pushing slightly past your comfortable pace), endurance training (longer sessions), and eliminating the last remaining hesitation points on specific keys or letter combinations. Regular [typing tests](/typing-test) to track your trend are essential at this stage.

**If you're above 80 WPM:** You're already proficient by professional standards. Further improvement is possible but requires increasingly deliberate effort. Competition-level typists at this range typically use custom training regimens focused on specific n-gram patterns and rhythm optimization.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good typing speed for the average person?

For general everyday use — emails, messages, browsing, casual documents — anything above 40 WPM is functional. A genuinely comfortable speed for most computer users is 55–65 WPM. At that level, typing rarely feels like a bottleneck. If you type frequently for work or study, pushing toward 70+ WPM is worth the effort.

### Is 40 WPM a good typing speed?

40 WPM is the global average, so it's "normal" — but it depends on your context. For occasional typing, 40 WPM is fine. For office work involving regular document production, it's on the low end. For typing-intensive roles like data entry or transcription, 40 WPM is below the minimum requirement. If you type for work regularly, targeting 60+ WPM is a practical goal.

### Is 60 WPM fast?

60 WPM is above average and comfortably ahead of most untrained typists. For general office work, it's solid. For typing-heavy professions like transcription, data entry, or journalism, 60 WPM would be considered slow to average. So: 60 WPM is fast relative to the general population, but not by professional standards for roles where typing is a primary job function.

### Is 80 WPM good?

Yes — 80 WPM is very good by any standard. It puts you in the proficient tier, well above the national average, and meets or exceeds the minimum requirements for almost every professional role that lists a WPM requirement. For most knowledge workers, 80 WPM means typing is no longer a meaningful bottleneck in their day.

### What typing speed do I need to get a job?

It depends on the role. Most general office jobs expect 40–55 WPM. Administrative and executive assistant roles typically require 55–70 WPM. Data entry positions commonly need 60–80 WPM. Transcription and legal secretary roles often require 70–90 WPM. If a job posting lists a minimum WPM, take that as a hard requirement — many employers test applicants.

### How fast do professional typists type?

Professional typists — court reporters, legal secretaries, medical transcriptionists — typically type between 75 and 100 WPM on standard keyboards, with 95%+ accuracy. Court reporters use stenotype machines and operate at 225+ WPM, but that's an entirely different system from standard keyboard typing.

### Do programmers need to type fast?

Not as much as other professionals. Programmers spend more time reading, thinking, and debugging than they do typing. That said, typing faster does reduce friction during implementation-heavy work, and many experienced developers naturally reach 65–80 WPM from years of keyboard use. Accuracy with symbols, brackets, and special characters matters more in programming than raw WPM.

### Does typing speed decrease with age?

Gradually, yes. Raw typing speed typically peaks in the 20–30 age range and decreases slowly afterward. However, accuracy and consistency often hold up or even improve into the late 50s, because older typists make fewer impulsive errors. The practical impact of age-related speed decline is smaller than most people assume — and consistent typing practice significantly slows any decline.

### What is the fastest typing speed ever recorded?

Barbara Blackburn holds the Guinness World Record for sustained typing on a standard keyboard, maintaining 150 WPM for 50 minutes and reaching a peak of 212 WPM in shorter bursts. Using the Dvorak keyboard layout, she consistently outperformed QWERTY typists in competition. For QWERTY typists specifically, speeds above 150 WPM are extraordinarily rare and represent years of dedicated training.

### How do I know if my typing speed is accurate?

The most reliable method: take 3–5 standardized tests using text you haven't seen before, and average the results. Single-test scores have high variance. Also make sure the test is reporting Net WPM (which subtracts error penalties) rather than Gross WPM, since Gross WPM overstates your usable speed if your accuracy isn't high.
