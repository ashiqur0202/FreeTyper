## The Global Average: What the Research Actually Says

The most widely cited figure for average typing speed is **40 WPM**. It comes from decades of occupational studies, typing software datasets, and large-scale ergonomics research — and it's broadly accurate as a floor-level estimate.

The most rigorous large-scale study on typing behavior comes from researchers at Aalto University in Finland and the University of Cambridge. Their dataset covered **168,000 volunteers** across more than 200 countries, capturing **136 million keystrokes** through a standardized online typing test. This study remains the most detailed scientific analysis of real-world typing behavior ever published.

Key finding from that research: the **median typing speed among participants was around 52 WPM** — but that group was self-selected. People who voluntarily seek out a typing test tend to type more than the average person, so this figure skews upward compared to the true general population.

The conservative general-population estimate — accounting for people who rarely type, older adults, and non-touch typists — puts the realistic global average closer to **38–44 WPM**.

A more recent analysis of over 10 million audited typing tests (excluding sub-15-second "burst" tests and bot signatures) found a **cleaned-data median of 41.6 WPM**, which aligns well with the older research.

**The most defensible answer: the average adult types between 38 and 44 WPM, with a median close to 41–42 WPM at roughly 92% accuracy.**

## Why "40 WPM" Is Misleading on Its Own

Here's the problem with quoting "40 WPM" as the average: it collapses an enormous range of people into a single number.

The 40 WPM average includes:
- A 12-year-old learning to type on a school Chromebook
- A 68-year-old who rarely uses a computer
- A college student who types 8 hours a day
- A data entry specialist with 15 years of experience
- A retiree who sends two emails a week

Those people have almost nothing in common when it comes to typing. Averaging their speeds produces a number that doesn't accurately represent any of them.

More useful comparisons look at **subgroups** — people in your age range, in your profession, with your level of experience. That's what the rest of this article provides.

One more important nuance: **the people who seek out typing tests are faster than average.** Any statistics derived from voluntary typing test participation will overstate the general-population speed, because casual or slow typists rarely bother testing themselves. When you see a figure from a typing platform claiming "our users average 65 WPM," that's not the average person — that's the average motivated-enough-to-test person.

Percentile rankings are the most useful way to understand where a given WPM score sits relative to the broader population. Here's how common speeds translate to population percentiles:

A few observations from this distribution:

**The jump from average to above-average is achievable quickly.** Going from 40 WPM (50th percentile) to 60 WPM (76th percentile) puts you ahead of three-quarters of adults — and most people can make that jump in 4–8 weeks of consistent practice.

**70 WPM is the professional tipping point.** At 70 WPM, you're faster than roughly 9 in 10 people. You meet or exceed the requirements for nearly every office job that lists a minimum WPM. This is a realistic goal for any adult who commits to structured practice.

**100 WPM is genuinely rare.** Only around 2% of the general population types at 100 WPM or above. This is the territory of dedicated touch typists, competitive typing enthusiasts, and professionals for whom speed is a direct job requirement.

## Average Typing Speed by Skill Level

Before looking at age and profession, it helps to see how speed maps to typing technique and experience level:

| Skill Level | Technique | Average WPM | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete beginner | Hunt-and-peck, 1–2 fingers | 10–20 WPM | 80–88% |
| Developing | Hunt-and-peck, improved | 25–35 WPM | 85–92% |
| Average untrained adult | Mixed technique | 35–50 WPM | 90–94% |
| Basic touch typist | Touch typing, learning | 40–55 WPM | 91–95% |
| Competent touch typist | Full touch typing | 55–75 WPM | 94–97% |
| Proficient touch typist | Fluent touch typing | 75–95 WPM | 96–98% |
| Expert typist | Optimized touch typing | 95–120 WPM | 97–99% |
| Competition-level | Highly trained | 120–200+ WPM | 98–99%+ |

The most important skill threshold on this table is the transition from "mixed technique" to "basic touch typing." That shift — learning to use all ten fingers with eyes on the screen — is responsible for more speed improvement than any amount of practice with poor technique. Someone who learns proper touch typing and practices it consistently for 30 days will almost always surpass a hunt-and-peck typist who has been at a keyboard for years.

## Average Typing Speed by Age Group

Typing speed isn't constant across a lifetime. It follows a predictable arc: it rises sharply during childhood and adolescence, peaks in young adulthood, plateaus through the 30s and 40s, and gradually declines from the 50s onward.

| Age Group | Average WPM Range | Notable Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Children (6–10) | 5–15 WPM | Learning fundamental motor patterns |
| Preteens (11–13) | 15–30 WPM | Rapid improvement from school and gaming |
| Teens (14–17) | 35–55 WPM | High screen time drives natural development |
| Young Adults (18–24) | 50–70 WPM | Speed peak; high typing volume and neural plasticity |
| Adults (25–35) | 50–70 WPM | Speed stable; accuracy often improves |
| Adults (36–50) | 42–62 WPM | Gradual plateau; precision strengthens |
| Adults (51–65) | 35–55 WPM | Moderate slowdown; accuracy remains solid |
| Seniors (66–75) | 28–48 WPM | Noticeable speed reduction; fewer errors |
| Seniors (76+) | 18–35 WPM | Motor slowdown more significant |

A few things this data shows that most articles miss:

**The 18–35 window is the speed peak, but the 25–35 group often has better *effective* output.** Younger typists (teens and early 20s) type faster in raw terms, but their error rates are significantly higher. Adults in their late 20s and early 30s have typically internalized accuracy as a habit, which means their net WPM — what actually makes it to the page without corrections — is often comparable to or higher than faster but sloppier younger typists.

**Older adults don't lose as much as the numbers suggest.** A 55-year-old typing at 45 WPM with 97% accuracy produces more usable text per hour than a 22-year-old typing at 60 WPM with 88% accuracy. The raw speed gap is real, but the practical output gap is smaller than it looks.

**Typing speed is a perishable skill — but a recoverable one.** Adults who type regularly throughout their careers maintain speed far better than those who step away from keyboards for extended periods. The biggest drop-offs tend to happen during career transitions or retirement, not through normal aging.

## Average Typing Speed by Generation

Generational data captures how different cohorts developed their typing habits in entirely different technological environments — and tells a more nuanced story than age alone.

| Generation | Birth Years | Avg. WPM | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gen Z | 1997–2012 | 50–65 WPM | Strong mobile typists; variable keyboard proficiency |
| Millennials | 1981–1996 | 52–58 WPM | Came of age with home computers; generally solid keyboard typists |
| Gen X | 1965–1980 | 44–52 WPM | Many had formal typing education; wide range of proficiency |
| Baby Boomers | 1946–1964 | 38–46 WPM | Computers arrived later in careers; fewer errors than younger typists |
| Silent Generation | 1928–1945 | 22–38 WPM | Mixed; some typewriter-trained, others came to computers late |

Millennials edge out Gen Z in average keyboard speed despite Gen Z having grown up with more technology — largely because touchscreen typing doesn't transfer to keyboard proficiency. Gen Z typists who spent the majority of their adolescent communication on smartphones often have strong mobile typing speeds but variable desktop speeds.

The accuracy gap between generations is also notable. Research consistently shows that older typists — Baby Boomers in particular — make significantly fewer errors per 100 words than younger typists, even when typing more slowly. Slower, cleaner typing is a legitimate professional advantage in many contexts.

## Touch Typing vs. Hunt-and-Peck: The Speed Gap

One of the most striking statistics in typing research is the speed gap between touch typists and hunt-and-peck typists — and the ceiling that two-finger technique imposes.

| Technique | Average WPM | Practical Speed Ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Hunt-and-peck (1–2 fingers) | 25–35 WPM | ~50–55 WPM (exceptional cases) |
| Modified technique (3–6 fingers) | 35–50 WPM | ~65–70 WPM (rare) |
| Touch typing (all 10 fingers) | 50–70 WPM | 100+ WPM with practice |

The Aalto University research produced a counterintuitive finding: the number of fingers used doesn't fully predict typing speed. What actually predicts speed is:

**Consistent finger assignment** — using the same finger for the same key every time, allowing reliable motor memory to form. A typist who uses six fingers consistently can outperform one who uses ten inconsistently.

**Hand position stability** — keeping hands anchored close to the home row rather than drifting across the keyboard for every keypress. Fast typists move their hands minimally; slow typists move their hands constantly.

**Anticipatory movement** — beginning the motion toward the next key before the current keystroke is fully completed. Researchers call this "rollover typing," and it's surprisingly common among fast typists even in self-taught styles.

The practical implication: if you're using fewer than eight fingers with inconsistent assignments, the fastest path to improvement isn't more practice with your current technique. It's fixing the technique first.

## Accuracy Statistics: The Number Most People Ignore

Speed gets all the attention, but accuracy matters just as much — and sometimes more.

Here's what the data shows:

- Average adult typing accuracy: approximately **92%** (roughly 8 errors per 100 words)
- Most professional typing standards: minimum **95% accuracy**
- High-stakes roles (legal, medical): **98–99% accuracy** expected
- Average uncorrected error rate in large-scale studies: approximately **2.3%** — separate from errors caught and fixed in real time

The most important accuracy statistic: **typists at 90% accuracy lose approximately 30% of their effective output** to error correction, compared to what their raw WPM implies.

Consider two typists side by side:

- **Typist A:** 70 WPM at 90% accuracy. Producing 7 errors per minute, each requiring backspace and retype — constant interruption to flow.
- **Typist B:** 55 WPM at 98% accuracy. Barely pauses for corrections. Output flows cleanly.

In terms of clean, usable words produced per minute, Typist B frequently wins despite a 15 WPM speed deficit on paper.

This is why professional typing assessments almost universally report **Net WPM** — which subtracts error penalties — rather than Gross WPM. And it's why the consistent guidance from typing instructors is to prioritize accuracy first, then build speed on top of a clean foundation.

## How Typing Speed Has Changed Over Time

Average typing speeds have risen substantially over the past 30 years — and the reason is almost entirely structural, not individual.

In the early 1990s, typing was still a specialist skill. Most white-collar workers dictated documents for secretarial staff to type. Personal computers were present but not universal, and the percentage of adults who typed regularly enough to develop real speed was a small fraction of the workforce.

The widespread adoption of PCs through the 1990s and internet connectivity through the 2000s changed this fundamentally. Two generations of workers have now grown up with keyboards as a daily tool, producing a consistent rise in average typing speed across the workforce.

The smartphone era introduced a new dimension: Aalto University research found that mobile typing speeds are rapidly closing the gap with desktop keyboard speeds — driven primarily by the two-thumb technique, which over 74% of smartphone users employ. However, desktop and mobile typing remain distinct skills with limited transfer between platforms.

The practical takeaway: the "average typist" of 2026 is faster than the average typist of 1996. As baseline averages rise, what qualifies as "above average" shifts upward too — which is one more reason not to be satisfied with just matching the current average.

## How to Find Your Own WPM

If you don't know your current typing speed, here's how to get an accurate, reliable baseline.

**Take multiple tests, not just one.** A single test has high variance — you can catch yourself on an unusually good or bad run. Take 3–5 tests across different days and average the results. That average is your real baseline.

**Use a standardized test length.** One minute is most common and convenient for benchmarking, but it measures burst speed rather than sustained output. Also test at 2–5 minutes to see how your speed holds up with fatigue.

**Use unfamiliar text.** Re-using the same test passage skews your score upward because you're partly working from short-term memory. Use a test that randomizes its text each time.

**Record both WPM and accuracy.** A speed number without accuracy is half the picture at best.

**Test at a consistent time of day.** Your typing speed varies with energy and focus. Testing at the same time across sessions gives you genuinely comparable data.

## What to Do If Your Speed Is Below Average

If your results came back below 40 WPM, here's the honest assessment: your typing technique is almost certainly the limiting factor, not your potential.

The vast majority of below-average typists are in that position for one or more of these reasons:
- Still looking at the keyboard while typing
- Using two to four fingers instead of all ten
- No consistent finger-to-key assignment
- Never having learned or practiced touch typing

Every one of these is fixable. None of them require exceptional ability. They require learning correct technique and practicing it consistently — and they typically produce dramatic improvement within weeks, not months.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the average typing speed for adults?

The most reliable estimate from large-scale research puts the global average adult typing speed at **38–44 WPM**, with a cleaned-data median around 41–42 WPM at approximately 92% accuracy. Studies drawn from voluntary testing platforms tend to report higher averages (50–52 WPM) because self-selected test-takers type more than the general population.

### Is 40 WPM above average or below?

Right at the average. Typing 40 WPM puts you at approximately the 50th percentile — exactly in the middle of the adult population. It's functional for everyday use, but below what most typing-intensive jobs require.

### What percentage of people type over 60 WPM?

Based on percentile distribution data from large-scale typing platforms, approximately **24% of adult typists** reach 60 WPM or above. That means 60 WPM places you in the top quarter of the adult population — solidly above average by any measure.

### What percentage of people type over 100 WPM?

Approximately **2% of the adult population** types at 100 WPM or above. Reaching this level requires sustained deliberate practice, proper touch typing technique fully internalized, and typically years of regular keyboard use.

### How does typing speed differ between generations?

Millennials average slightly higher keyboard speeds than Gen Z (around 52–58 WPM vs. 50–65 WPM), despite Gen Z growing up with more technology overall. The reason: heavy smartphone use in adolescence develops mobile typing skills that don't transfer well to desktop keyboard speed. Baby Boomers average lower raw speeds but make significantly fewer errors, giving them strong practical output despite the WPM gap.

### Do mechanical keyboards make you type faster?

For trained touch typists, mechanical keyboards with tactile feedback can provide a modest speed benefit — the physical confirmation of each keystroke reduces unconscious hesitation. However, the effect is small compared to the impact of technique and practice. Keyboard choice is a fine-tuning decision, not a meaningful lever for most typists below 70 WPM.

### Is typing speed declining because of smartphones?

The evidence doesn't support this. Average keyboard typing speeds have been stable or slightly rising across the workforce. Smartphone use has shifted how some people communicate, but desktop keyboard use for professional and academic work has remained consistent. Mobile and keyboard typing are distinct enough skills that heavy smartphone use doesn't meaningfully degrade desktop proficiency.

### How long would it take to go from 40 WPM to 60 WPM?

For most adults committing to daily structured practice, the jump from 40 to 60 WPM takes approximately **4 to 8 weeks**. The biggest accelerator is switching to proper touch typing if you aren't already using all ten fingers. If you're already a touch typist, focused drills on weak spots and daily consistency will get you there in the same timeframe.

### What is the world record for typing speed?

Barbara Blackburn holds the Guinness World Record for sustained typing on a standard keyboard, reaching a peak of **212 WPM** in short bursts and sustaining **150 WPM** over 50 minutes, using a Dvorak keyboard layout. On QWERTY in modern competitive typing, top competitors regularly sustain 150+ WPM on short texts. These figures are not meaningful benchmarks for professional or everyday typing goals.

### How accurate is a typing speed test?

A single 1-minute test has notable variance. For a reliable benchmark, take 3–5 tests across different sessions and average them. Tests that report Net WPM (subtracting error penalties) give a more honest picture of productive speed than Gross WPM. Consistent testing conditions — same time of day, same duration, unfamiliar text — produce the most comparable data over time.
